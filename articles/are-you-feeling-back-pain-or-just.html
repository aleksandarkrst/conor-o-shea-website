Are you feeling back pain or just predicting it?

27 Nov 2026 · 3 min read · Written by Conor O'Shea · Conor O'Shea Fitness

You’ve had a great week in the gym.

You’re deadlifting, squatting, and moving better than you have in months.

Then you bend down to pick up the laundry basket.

Suddenly, your back feels weak again.

And the questions start.

Have I hurt myself? Is my disc getting worse? Is another flare-up coming?

If this sounds familiar, there’s something worth knowing.

Your brain doesn’t just react to what’s happening. It also tries to predict what will happen next.

Think about the last time you picked up something heavy.

Before you even touched it, you had an idea of how heavy it would feel.

That guess came from all the times you’ve lifted something like it before.

Your brain uses past experiences to help make sense of what you’re feeling today.

Most of the time, that’s helpful.

But if you’ve had a bad back flare-up, your past can also make you fear certain movements.

Imagine two people doing the same deadlift.

Both are strong enough to lift 60 kg.

The first has been lifting for ten years. He walks up to the bar feeling confident.

The second hurt his back deadlifting two years ago. He’s had several flare-ups since.

He walks up to the same bar wondering if it’s going to happen again.

Same bar. Same weight. Same strength.

But a very different experience.

That doesn’t mean the second person’s pain is in his head.

His pain is real. But his past may also shape how he feels about lifting today.

Maybe he bent forward one morning and had a terrible flare-up.

For months after, he avoided bending. Every bit of tightness made him worry.

Over time, he started to link bending with danger.

Even when his back improved, that fear stayed.

His brain may still expect the worst because of what happened before.

But what happened last time doesn’t always tell you what will happen today.

So how do you change that?

Not by avoiding movement forever.

And not by pushing through everything either.

You start with a movement you can manage.

Maybe that’s a simple hip hinge with no weight.

Then you bend a little further.

Then you try a light kettlebell.

Over time, you build toward heavier lifting.

You’re getting stronger, but you’re also gaining new experiences.

You expected the hinge to hurt, but it didn’t.

You thought the kettlebell might cause a flare-up, but you felt fine the next day.

Or maybe you felt some pain, made a small change, and things settled down.

Each experience gives you more information about what your back can handle.

Research supports this kind of step-by-step rehab. The idea that it works by changing your brain’s predictions is still being studied.

One thing to watch out for.

You don’t want to spend all day checking your back for pain.

There’s a difference between being aware of your body and always looking for signs of danger.

Notice how you feel.

Change the weight or movement when you need to.

See how you feel later that day and the next.

Then get on with your day.

Being pain-free isn’t the only sign of progress.

Here’s something to try this week.

Pick one movement you’ve been nervous about.

Before you do it, ask yourself:

1. What do I think will happen?

Will it hurt? Will I have a flare-up tomorrow? Or am I just nervous?

2. What actually happened?

Try a version of the movement you can manage. Notice how you feel during and after it.

3. Was it different from what I expected?

Maybe it went better than you thought.

Or maybe you need to make the exercise easier.

Either way, you’ve learned something useful.

You only need to try this once or twice. Don’t turn it into another habit of checking every little feeling.

Because the goal isn’t to spend the rest of your life thinking about your back.

It’s to get back to living your life.
